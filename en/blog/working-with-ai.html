What AI Won't Do for Me

Aug 7, 2026 5 min read
AI DevOps Method

A good part of my site was written by an AI. I say it without embarrassment, because the interesting question is no longer whether AI can code for you: it already does, every day. The real question is what's left for you to do, and how you keep control without kidding yourself.

I have an answer, necessarily partial, and my site is a good example. I started it knowing neither HTML nor CSS; it is far more polished today than at its beginnings, and it lets me explain, concretely, how I work with AI.

Understand before delegating

I don't delegate what I've never understood, even a little. That's my first rule, and it's where the site began.

At first, I wanted to learn HTML and CSS. I designed a mockup in Figma, then built it by hand, almost without JavaScript. It was slow, sometimes clumsy, but every line showed me how a page holds together. Reproducing a mockup when you're discovering CSS is a good lesson in humility: you quickly realize that even centering an element properly means understanding what's happening underneath. I didn't come out of it an expert, only able to understand the basics and use them. That was the point.

Why bother, since the AI would have churned all that out in minutes? Because a tool you don't understand ends up owning you. The day it breaks, or the day you have to explain a choice, you're stuck. Understanding the foundations, even modestly, is what later lets you delegate without becoming a hostage to what you delegated.

Judgment first, code second

Once the basics are in place, the AI becomes a very fast executor. But an executor needs someone to decide, and that's where my work is: almost entirely upstream and downstream of the code.

When I wanted to move the site from a student project to something more professional, I didn't just throw "make me a nice site" into the void. I set a clear idea, guidelines, a frame. My guidelines come down to a few explicit constraints: no framework, reuse the components already there, keep the site fast and readable. The AI works within that frame, not beside it. I weighed the pros and cons of the options. The AI coded, I tested, I iterated, and I only published when the result matched what I wanted. The site's tech watch, on the other hand, that pipeline publishing an edition every week, I coded almost entirely myself: I only handed the AI the regeneration of the HTML pages, the repetitive part. That's my turf, automation, so I get my hands in it.

This is maybe what AI changes most about the job. Making something is no longer necessarily writing it yourself; it's deciding it, framing it, and knowing how to recognize when it's good. The keyboard matters less, the judgment more.

Control the result, not every line

I don't read every line of what the AI produces. My site's JavaScript, for instance, I haven't combed through. What I do know is what to test to check that a change works and hasn't broken anything else. My control is on the behavior, not on each instruction. Concretely, whenever the AI touches the CSS or the JavaScript, I always check the same points: the display in light and dark, on mobile as on desktop, in both languages, and that an unrelated page hasn't shifted. No need to read the diff line by line for that; you need to know what can break, and go look at it.

That doesn't mean skimming everything. The level of understanding I demand of myself depends on the domain. On the front end, I accept delegating and not mastering everything; that's not where I focus my attention. On DevOps and cloud, it's different: there, even without knowing the code by heart, I want to understand what I'm doing and why, because it's my turf and I'll be judged on it. Knowing where to set the cursor of your understanding is already a decision.

Don't lie about what you did

The last rule is the simplest and the most important: honestly saying what comes from me and what comes from the AI.

It's not just a matter of principle. One day, in an interview or in front of a colleague, you'll have to defend these choices and explain what's running. Anyone who passes off as their own code they neither wrote nor understood gets exposed in three questions. You only defend well what you truly own.

This honesty has a useful side effect: it forces you to know exactly where you stand. What you master, what you delegate, what you should dig into. It's uncomfortable, and that's precisely why it's healthy.

What it comes down to

Working this way doesn't make me a better coder. It makes me someone who uses AI without dissolving into it: faster on what isn't my core, focused on what matters, and still able to explain and evolve what I ship.

AI hasn't changed what I expect of myself. It has only forced me to be clear about what I master, what I delegate, and what I still have to learn. It will always write code faster than me; it won't understand the problem in my place, and it won't choose the right solution for my context. That's still where the job is, and that's where I want to get good.

This site is the first place I practice it, out in the open. The code is public, if you want to see: github.com/TuroTheReal/Portfolio.
